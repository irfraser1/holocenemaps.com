1680–1780

By the late 17th century, France was staking bold claims across North America. Within 100 years they had ceded it all to the British and Spanish empires. This map collection tells the story of 1680–1780 America — a century of ambition, contest and resolution.

The Maps

Act I · 1719

Henri Abraham Chatelain

Carte de la Nouvelle France, ou se voit le cours des grandes rivières

1719 · Amsterdam

This map was produced in 1719 by Henri Chatelain in his Atlas Historique, a commercial atlas aimed at educated European readers. Drawing on De l'Isle's authoritative 1718 cartography, it presents French territorial claims over the Mississippi basin and Great Lakes at the precise moment the Mississippi Bubble was at its peak.

Henri Chatelain was born in 1684 to a French Huguenot family driven into exile in Amsterdam after Louis XIV made Protestantism illegal, forcing hundreds of thousands from France. This stands in stark contrast to De l'Isle, who served as Premier Géographe du Roi — Geographer Royal to Louis XV — working at the very heart of French royal power. That the most authoritative French imperial map of the period and Chatelain's lavish elaboration of it were produced by men at opposite ends of French society remains one of the more remarkable footnotes of the era.

While not a deliberate investment instrument, it was published for an audience gripped by Louisiana fever and the promise of vast economic opportunity in the French interior. Its rich decoration made French imperial ambition feel vivid, real, and vast to the European readership consuming it.

Act II · The Imperial Contest

Emanuel Bowen

A Map of the King of Great Britain's Dominions in Europe, Africa, and America

c. 1740 · London

Produced on the eve of the French and Indian War, this map makes British imperial claim its explicit subject. Yellow outlining identifies territories possessed by the King of Great Britain. The Atlantic is framed as a British lake — a statement of what Britain believed it owned, and was prepared to defend.

Act III · British Resolution

John Gibson · The Gentleman's Magazine

The British Governments in North America, Agreeable to the Proclamation of October 7, 1763

1763 · London

Published to accompany the Royal Proclamation of 1763, this map codifies Britain's victory over France in visual form. The vast territory labeled "LANDS RESERVED for the INDIANS" marks the Proclamation Line — the moment of British resolution, and the seed of the coming revolution.

Railroad Promotion · 1909

Poole Brothers · The Texas Realty Journal

Iron Mountain Route to All Parts of Texas

1909 · Houston, Texas

A railroad promotion map advertising round-trip homeseeker rates on the Iron Mountain Route. The map annotates the state by agricultural product and land use — cotton, corn, cattle, oil fields — making it as much an economic argument as a geographic document. A vivid artifact of the era's land promotion culture.

Fortune Magazine · 1939

Richard Edes Harrison

The City of New York

July 1939 · Fortune Magazine

One of the most celebrated American maps of the twentieth century. Harrison's oblique aerial projection was revolutionary — presenting the city as if seen from an aircraft at a time when such a view was still extraordinary. It would go on to define wartime cartography in American media.
